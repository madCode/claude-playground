# Training notes

What the research says about the rules the coach uses, for anyone changing one. Checked on
2026-11-22: every source was opened and compared with what it is cited for. "(abstract)" means
only the abstract was read. Newer evidence replaces an entry; it doesn't stack beside it.

How sure: **several agree** (more than one meta-analysis), **thin** (consistent, few or small
studies), **reasoned** (inferred from neighbouring evidence; nothing tests it).

Nearly all of it is lab sets with full rest, mostly in young men. None is from circuit classes
with dumbbells in fixed steps. Treat every rule here as a sensible default, not a proven optimum.

## 1. When to raise the weight

- **No trial compares "up every time" with "up after two sessions" or "up when it felt easy".**
  The two-session rule is expert consensus (reasoned). ACSM: raise the load 2–10% "when the
  individual can perform the current workload for one to two repetitions over the desired number
  on two consecutive training sessions" (their evidence grade B). The NSCA's "2-for-2" says the
  same of the last set; its wording was confirmed only in a study guide, not the textbook.
- **Adding reps at the same weight works about as well as adding weight** over 8–10 weeks, in
  trained and untrained people (thin: two trials, leg exercises). This is what "work up through
  the range, then go up" (double progression) rests on, and it suits a rack whose next step is
  far away.
- **Loading by feel (RPE or reps in reserve) is about as good as fixed plans**, with at most a
  small edge (thin: one meta-analysis of six small studies, trained lifters only).
- **Nothing studies the size of the jump.** The 2–10% in the guidance is far below this rack's
  17–25% steps (10→12, 12→15, 20→25). By Epley's arithmetic, not a study: about 12 reps at a
  weight predicts about 8 at one 10% heavier, but only 4 or 5 at one 20–25% heavier.

For Spotter: go up on stronger evidence than one ticked session (two in a row is the
consensus); where there is a range, reach its top first; before a big jump, expect either more
reps than the board asks or a drop in reps after it, which is what the "try 15 if set 1 felt
easy" test number is for. DESIGN.md, "Progression".

- Ratamess et al. 2009, ACSM position stand, doi:10.1249/MSS.0b013e3181915670 (full text): the
  two-consecutive-sessions, 2–10% rule.
- Plotkin et al. 2022, doi:10.7717/peerj.14142 (full text): trained lifters, 8 weeks; adding
  reps and adding load gave similar strength and size.
- Chaves et al. 2024, doi:10.1055/a-2256-5857 (abstract): 39 untrained people, 10 weeks; no
  difference between the two.
- Hickmott et al. 2022, doi:10.1186/s40798-021-00404-9 (full text, first half): loading by feel
  against percentages, 1RM difference 2.07 kg (95% CI −0.32 to 4.46).

## 2. How hard a set should be

- **Stopping short of failure costs little.** Strength is the same; muscle growth edges up
  nearer failure (several agree). No study tests "1–2 reps in reserve" against failure
  directly: that figure is an inference. In trained people failure was slightly better for
  growth (Grgic).
- **Failure slows recovery**, by up to 24–48 hours (thin: ten trained men, and the comparison
  set stopped far from failure).

For Spotter: a near miss holds; it is not a fault to fix. The cut-offs (hold when one or two
short, drop when three short, on a short first set, broken form or a stop) are common coaching
practice, not tested thresholds.

- Refalo et al. 2023, doi:10.1007/s40279-022-01784-y (abstract): failure against not, effect
  0.19 (95% CI 0.00–0.37) for any definition of failure; 0.12 and not significant for strict
  failure.
- Robinson et al. 2024, doi:10.1007/s40279-024-02069-2 (abstract): strength similar across
  reps in reserve; growth rises nearer failure. The authors call it exploratory.
- Grgic et al. 2022, doi:10.1016/j.jshs.2021.01.007 (abstract): 15 studies; no overall
  difference for strength (−0.09) or growth (0.22, not significant).
- Morán-Navarro et al. 2017, PMID 28965198 (abstract): recovery.
- Lopez et al. 2021, doi:10.1249/MSS.0000000000002585 (abstract): 28 studies; growth is
  similar across loads taken to failure, strength favours heavier loads.

## 3. "Felt easy", "felt hard"

- **People have more reps left than they think**, by about one on average, and by two to five
  in long sets or when they call it far from failure (several agree on the direction: one
  meta-analysis and consistent trials). Experience helps little.
- **Self-chosen weights run light**: about half of the one-rep max on average (one
  meta-analysis).
- **Nothing tests whether "felt hard" should stop an increase** (reasoned). A feeling recalled
  after a circuit class is noisier than the in-set ratings studied.

For Spotter: "felt easy" is a fair reason to go up, since the error runs toward having more in
the tank. "Felt hard" on a set that reached its reps is weak evidence: it delays a step, it
doesn't cancel one. The reps done decide; the feel breaks ties.

- Halperin et al. 2022, doi:10.1007/s40279-021-01559-x (abstract): 12 studies, 414 people;
  under-predicted by 0.95 reps (95% CI 0.17–1.73); better near failure and at 12 reps or fewer.
- Zourdos et al. 2021, PMID 30747900 (abstract): errors of 2.05, 3.65 and 5.15 reps when 1, 3
  and 5 in reserve were called, in sets of about 16.
- Steele et al. 2022, doi:10.1007/s40279-022-01717-9 (abstract): self-chosen loads average 53%
  of the one-rep max.
- Gómez-Redondo et al. 2025, doi:10.1016/j.exger.2025.112884 (abstract): 25 older adults
  misjudged by about two reps near failure. The direction is unclear from the abstract: don't
  cite it for direction.

## 4. Ladders and drop sets

- **Nothing says which round to progress from** (reasoned).
- **The shape doesn't change the result**: with the same total work, rising ladders, drop sets
  and ordinary sets give the same strength and size (several agree for drop sets; one trial for
  rising ladders). Drop sets feel harder. "Straight sets are better for strength" is only
  possible: the pooled results show no difference, and the one study in favour had 8 people a
  group.

For Spotter: pick one anchor and keep it. The heaviest round is judged against its own round's
reps (the "4" of "8 / 6 / 4"), never converted to an 8-rep weight (see 5). A drop set would count
from its first, heaviest part; the coach doesn't read drop sets yet.

- Angleri et al. 2017, doi:10.1007/s00421-016-3529-1 (abstract): ladders, drop sets and
  ordinary sets, 12 weeks, same gains.
- Sødal et al. 2023, doi:10.1186/s40798-023-00620-5 (full text): drop sets, growth only.
- Havers et al. 2026, doi:10.1186/s40798-026-01012-1 (full text): 9 studies of long-term
  results; growth 0.04, strength −0.04, neither significant.
- Coleman et al. 2022, doi:10.47206/ijsc.v2i1.135 (full text): 5 studies; trivial differences.
  The time saved comes from two of the five, not a pooled figure.

## 5. Converting a weight between rep counts (Epley)

- **Sound to about 10 reps, worse beyond**, and already less accurate at 10 than at 5 (thin
  for the cut-off; one large meta-regression for the shape).
- **It differs by lift and by person.** At 80% of the max, about 9 reps on a bench press and
  about 13 on a leg press, with a spread of two to four reps between people: as much as one
  dumbbell step. No study checks it on dumbbell lifts.

For Spotter: a conversion is a first guess, rounded down, for the lift's own history to correct;
never the basis for a step up in the same move. Keep each exercise's history at its own rep
count rather than one estimated max.

- Reynolds et al. 2006, J Strength Cond Res 20(3):584–592 (full text): "no more than 10
  repetitions should be used"; leg press and chest press only.
- Nuzzo et al. 2024, doi:10.1007/s40279-023-01937-7 (full text, first part): 269 studies; the
  reps at each load by lift, and the spread.

## 6. A twinge

- **All the evidence is from rehab of long-standing problems** (tendons, heel, low back), not
  a new twinge in a healthy person. There, exercising into moderate pain does no harm (thin: one
  meta-analysis of seven trials). The usual rule: up to 5 out of 10 during, settled by the next
  morning.
- **Nothing tests holding the weight against lowering it** (reasoned).

For Spotter: "a hurt set never raises a number" and "a twinge holds, had to stop drops a step"
are madCode's decisions and cautious practice, not research findings. The models would also
ask whether it was gone by the next morning, which the app doesn't. Not medical advice.

- Smith et al. 2017, doi:10.1136/bjsports-2016-097383 (full text, first part).
- Silbernagel et al. 2007, doi:10.1177/0363546506298279 (abstract).

## 7. One leg, one arm

- **Two limbs together often lift a little less than the sum of each alone**, but not
  reliably (thin). Gains are specific: one-leg training improves one-leg strength most.
- **A rear-foot-elevated split squat came in at about half the back squat's bar weight** in one
  small group of rugby players (thin, and partly arithmetic). No study measures one-arm against
  two-arm dumbbell presses.

For Spotter: the 0.6 one-leg factor is a coaching estimate with no source; half would be the
more cautious seed. Keep each version's own history and let the first logs correct any seed.
Strength per movement (step 5) should be judged on the scorecard for this reason.

- Škarabot et al. 2016, doi:10.1007/s00421-016-3458-z (abstract).
- Speirs et al. 2016, doi:10.1519/JSC.0000000000001096 (full text).
- Kassiano et al. 2025, doi:10.1007/s40279-024-02169-z (abstract).

## 8. What a swing trains

A kettlebell swing is a hip hinge: glutes and hamstrings do most of the work (thin: three small
EMG studies). Reasoned for a dumbbell skier swing.

- McGill & Marshall 2012, doi:10.1519/JSC.0b013e31823a4063; Zebis et al. 2013,
  doi:10.1136/bjsports-2011-090281; Van Gelder et al. 2015, PMID 26618061 (abstracts).

## 9. Coming back to a weight after time away

- **With no training at all, strength holds for about three to four weeks and then falls**,
  more the longer the break (several agree on the shape; the sizes vary a lot). One review of
  elite contact-sport athletes found strength kept "for up to 3 weeks" and an average loss of
  about 14% over breaks averaging seven weeks.
- **What is lost comes back fast**: after ten weeks off, novices regained their strength within
  five weeks of training (thin: one trial).
- **A small dose keeps it**: one session a week, at the same relative load, held strength for
  up to 32 weeks (thin: one trial and a narrative review).
- **Nothing measures a lift left out while the same muscles go on training**, which is this
  app's case. That strength lasts well beyond eight weeks is reasoned from the line above; the
  skill of the lift itself (balance, coordination) isn't measured by anything found.
- **Every source counts in weeks, none in sessions.**

For Spotter: a heavier weight done clean vouches for a lighter one come back to for eight
weeks, madCode's choice (2026-11-23). It is on the cautious side for someone still training,
and costs little: after it, the lighter weight goes up on its own two clean sessions. Weeks,
not classes, as the research counts. The eight weeks run between the heavier session and the
lighter one. A step already earned doesn't expire, however long the lift then goes undone:
whether it should is madCode's to say (BACKLOG, "Your call"). DESIGN.md, "Progression".

All abstracts only, but for Rowe:

- McMaster et al. 2013, doi:10.1007/s40279-013-0031-3: the three weeks and the 14%.
- Bosquet et al. 2013, doi:10.1111/sms.12047: 103 studies; loss grows with the break.
- Halonen et al. 2024, doi:10.1111/sms.14739: ten weeks off, regained in five.
- Bickel et al. 2011, doi:10.1249/MSS.0b013e318207c15d; Spiering et al. 2021,
  doi:10.1519/JSC.0000000000003964: the maintenance dose.
- Hwang et al. 2017, doi:10.1519/JSC.0000000000001807; Ogasawara et al. 2011,
  doi:10.1111/j.1475-097X.2011.01031.x: no loss after two or three weeks.
- Rowe et al. 2024, doi:10.1007/s00421-024-05509-z (full text): 1RM unchanged after four weeks.

## Leads, not conclusions

- The NSCA textbook's exact 2-for-2 wording.
- Any trial on the size of the jump, or on progression with fixed-step dumbbells.
- Reps-in-reserve ratings recalled after a session, and in circuits with short rests.
- The cardio blocks' effect on strength gains; women and older adults.
- Results of the "pain allowed against pain avoided" trial (Kjær et al. 2024 protocol,
  doi:10.1186/s13063-024-07973-6).
